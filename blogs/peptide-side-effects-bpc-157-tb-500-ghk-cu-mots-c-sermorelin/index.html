Peptide Side Effects: What You Should Know About BPC-157, TB-500, GHK-Cu, MOTS-C, and Sermorelin

A practical, evidence-first guide to side effects, unanswered safety questions, and when to seek medical help for five commonly discussed peptides.

·By Matt Aertker·November 14, 2026·11 min read

People researching peptides often ask a reasonable question: “What side effects should I actually expect?” Unfortunately, the most useful answer is not the same for every peptide. Some medicines made from peptides have years of human clinical data and FDA-approved labeling. Others are still being investigated, with too little human evidence to say which effects are common, rare, or possible over the long term.

This guide focuses on five names that come up frequently in recovery and wellness conversations: BPC-157, TB-500, GHK-Cu, MOTS-C, and sermorelin. We'll look at what researchers hope to learn, what has actually been observed in humans, and which safety questions remain unanswered. The goal is to help you ask better questions, not to persuade you that any of these substances is right for you.

But there is an important boundary: our nonclinical support team does not diagnose symptoms, evaluate whether a side effect is dangerous for you, or make treatment decisions. Those questions go to a licensed medical professional. If you're still working out which peptides are worth researching at all, start with our guide to choosing the right peptide for your goals.

First, what does “peptide side effect” mean?

A peptide is a chain of amino acids. That's a description of its structure, not a guarantee of safety. Different peptides interact with different biological pathways and have different levels of human research behind them.

When you see a list of alleged side effects online, separate three categories:

  • Observed in people: A symptom or adverse event has been reported in clinical use or human research. This does not always prove the substance caused it, but it is more directly relevant than a theory.
  • Plausible or identified risk: A mechanism, animal finding, or product-quality concern suggests something that needs investigation. It is not the same as a measured rate of side effects in people.
  • Unknown: Human studies have not been large, long, or rigorous enough to establish what happens. “No side effects reported” in a very small study does not mean “no side effects.”

The FDA has specifically flagged concerns about peptide-related impurities, immune reactions, and limited human safety information for several substances discussed here. Its compounding safety assessment (opens in a new tab) is an essential starting point.

BPC-157 side effects: what do human studies show?

BPC-157 is a synthetic peptide studied mainly in laboratory and animal models, often in connection with tissue repair and inflammatory processes. That research helps explain the interest in BPC-157, but it cannot establish that the peptide heals human injuries or is safe for routine human use. Beema Health offers compounded BPC-157 injections and BPC-157 capsules. Route matters: the research below does not tell you how an oral capsule compares with an injection, so treat the two as separate questions for your clinician.

A 2026 systematic review of human BPC-157 evidence (opens in a new tab) identified only three studies involving 30 people in total. Those studies reported no treatment-related adverse events, but researchers judged their methods poor and the certainty of the evidence very low. With so few people studied, rare adverse events and longer-term consequences could easily go undetected.

What is known about BPC-157 safety?

  • Established frequency of side effects: Not available from sufficiently reliable human trials.
  • Possible concerns: The FDA has identified potential immune reactions (immunogenicity, meaning an unwanted immune response), peptide-related impurities, and difficulties in characterizing the active ingredient in compounded products.
  • Major unknowns: Appropriate human dosing, interactions, safety in different patient populations, and long-term outcomes.

The takeaway is not that BPC-157 is proven dangerous or proven harmless. It is that the evidence is not strong enough to make either claim. The FDA's safety-risk discussion (opens in a new tab) reflects this uncertainty.

TB-500 side effects: why product identity matters

TB-500 is commonly discussed in the context of wound healing and recovery. However, some articles blur the distinction between TB-500, a fragment associated with thymosin beta-4, and studies of full-length thymosin beta-4. Evidence about one substance or formulation cannot automatically be transferred to the other. Beema Health offers TB-500 only as part of a combined BPC-157, TB-500, and GHK-Cu injection, and combinations have even less human safety research than single ingredients.

The FDA describes the substance it evaluated as thymosin beta-4, fragment (LKKTETQ), also known as TB-500. In its review, the FDA said it had not identified human exposure data for drug products containing that fragment. It highlighted possible immune responses related to aggregation or peptide impurities and said it lacked important information about human harm. See the FDA's ingredient-specific assessment (opens in a new tab).

What is known about TB-500 safety?

  • Established frequency of side effects: Unknown for the evaluated TB-500 fragment.
  • Possible concerns: Immune reactions, peptide aggregation, impurities, and the general risks of nonsterile or improperly prepared injectable products.
  • Major unknowns: Human safety with repeated use, medication interactions, and whether findings from other thymosin-related substances apply at all.

A 2026 sports-medicine review (opens in a new tab) found that enthusiasm for BPC-157 and thymosin-related products has outpaced convincing human research on musculoskeletal recovery. It is reasonable to follow the science, but not to treat unproven recovery claims as established medical benefits.

GHK-Cu side effects: topical and injectable use are different questions

GHK-Cu is a copper-binding peptide that appears in skincare research and some topical cosmetic products. Its interest in skin biology does not establish that injecting GHK-Cu is safe or effective. Beema Health offers compounded GHK-Cu injections, not a cosmetic serum.

What about GHK-Cu creams or serums?

With a topical product, someone may experience local irritation or sensitivity depending on the complete formulation. Product-specific cosmetic tolerability is a separate question from systemic drug safety. Preliminary skin-related studies should not be used to promise wound healing, muscle recovery, or other outcomes from injections.

What about injectable GHK-Cu?

For injectable GHK-Cu, the FDA identifies limited human safety data and a potential for immune reactions related to peptide aggregation and impurities. It has not established reliable rates for adverse effects of compounded injectable GHK-Cu. See FDA's GHK-Cu entry (opens in a new tab).

An important practical question is therefore not simply “Does GHK-Cu have side effects?” but “Which exact formulation and route of use are we talking about, and what data support that route?” A cosmetic serum and a compounded injection should never be treated as interchangeable.

MOTS-C side effects: promising biology, very limited human answers

MOTS-C is a mitochondrial-derived peptide being studied for its relationship to cellular energy regulation and metabolism. Laboratory and animal findings make it an interesting research subject, but they do not establish clinical benefit or a safe human-use profile. Beema Health offers compounded MOTS-C injections.

In its published safety assessment, the FDA said it had not identified human exposure data for drug products containing MOTS-C administered by any route. It noted potential immunogenicity and ingredient-characterization concerns and said it lacked information about whether MOTS-C could harm people. See FDA's MOTS-C assessment (opens in a new tab).

What is known about MOTS-C safety?

  • Established frequency of side effects: Not known from a reliable human adverse-event dataset.
  • Possible concerns: Unwanted immune responses and problems related to impurities or product characterization.
  • Major unknowns: Human efficacy, organ-system effects, interactions, and both short- and long-term tolerability.

You may see confident online descriptions of what MOTS-C “feels like.” Anecdotes are not a substitute for controlled human safety studies, especially when the preparation and dosage may differ between users.

Sermorelin side effects: what is better documented?

Sermorelin differs from the preceding substances because it has a history of human medical use. It is an analog of growth hormone-releasing hormone: it signals the pituitary gland to release growth hormone. The branded product Geref was previously FDA-approved for specific pediatric and diagnostic uses and later discontinued. The FDA determined it was not withdrawn for reasons of safety or effectiveness. That historical determination is not a new approval, nor does it establish safety for adult anti-aging or body-composition use. See the FDA drug-approval record (opens in a new tab).

Reported sermorelin side effects

According to Mayo Clinic's sermorelin drug information (opens in a new tab), reported effects include:

  • More commonly reported: Pain, redness, or swelling at the injection site.
  • Less commonly or rarely reported: Headache, flushing, dizziness, sleepiness, or restlessness.
  • Symptoms requiring prompt medical assessment: Itching or trouble swallowing, which may indicate a reaction that needs medical attention.

These descriptions come from historical drug information. They are not measured event rates for Beema Health's compounded sermorelin preparation, and the safety of a current compounded product should not be assumed identical to the former branded drug.

For adults considering sermorelin for wellness or body composition, the key additional issue is limited evidence for those specific goals. It would be misleading to treat an older pediatric approval as proof of anti-aging benefits in adults. Risks that can matter across injectable peptides

Even when a peptide's direct pharmacological side effects are not well defined, the way a product is prepared and administered introduces separate considerations.

Product quality and contamination

Poor compounding practices can lead to contamination or incorrect ingredient strength. The FDA explains (opens in a new tab) that these problems can cause serious injury. A label or online claim of “purity” does not replace appropriate pharmacy quality controls.

Injection-site reactions and infection

Pain, redness, and swelling can occur after injections, but spreading redness, increasing warmth or pain, drainage, or fever warrants prompt medical evaluation. This is a general injection safety concern, not evidence that any one of these five peptides has a particular infection rate.

Allergic or immune reactions

The FDA has identified possible immunogenicity concerns for multiple compounded peptides. Whether and how often these reactions happen in people may be unknown. A suspected serious allergic reaction needs immediate attention.

Drug interactions and long-term effects

For several of these substances, the problem is not a documented list of dangerous interactions. The problem is that reliable human interaction studies and long-term follow-up are missing. That makes it especially important to discuss existing conditions, medications, supplements, allergies, and treatment goals with a licensed clinician rather than constructing a self-directed protocol.

When should you contact your clinician or seek emergency care?

Call 911 or seek emergency treatment immediately for trouble breathing; swelling of the tongue, throat, or face; fainting with signs of a severe reaction; chest pain; or another sudden, severe symptom. Do not rely on a support chat or routine doctor message for emergencies.

Seek prompt medical assessment for a rapidly worsening or possibly infected injection site, fever with increasing redness or drainage, trouble swallowing, or any new or severe symptom that concerns you. The right level of urgency depends on the symptoms and your medical history.

Contact your prescribing clinician about less urgent but persistent symptoms, such as ongoing headache, unusual dizziness, or injection-site irritation. Don't change the amount or frequency of a prescription based on an internet article. Ask the clinician who knows your health history.

How do you evaluate peptide safety before treatment?

Rather than looking for a universal “safe peptide” list, consider asking:

  • What exactly is the substance? Is it the specific compound described in human research, a related peptide, a fragment, or a combination?
  • What is the route? Topical, oral, and injectable products have different evidence and risks.
  • Is it FDA-approved? If it is compounded, why is compounding considered, and what does the relevant legal framework permit?
  • What human evidence exists for my goal? Look for controlled trials and clinically meaningful outcomes, not just animal findings or testimonials.
  • What are the known, plausible, and unknown risks? If no reliable side-effect frequencies exist, that uncertainty should be stated plainly.
  • Who makes and dispenses the medication? How can the pharmacy's licensing and product-quality practices be verified?
  • Who handles follow-up? How should you report symptoms, and what should prompt urgent care?

The FDA explains that compounded medications are not FDA-approved and are not independently reviewed for safety, effectiveness, and quality before marketing. In October 2026, HHS announced a limited, conditional enforcement-discretion policy for certain 503A compounded peptides, including BPC-157, TB-500, and MOTS-C. That policy does not approve these drugs or establish that they are safe or effective. It is also not a blanket permission to compound any peptide or any combination. Read the HHS policy (opens in a new tab) and the Federal Register notice (opens in a new tab) for the actual conditions.

Frequently asked questions

Are BPC-157 and TB-500 side effects well studied in humans?

No. The human research is too limited to estimate dependable side-effect rates. Small studies or the absence of reported events should not be mistaken for proof of safety.

Is injectable GHK-Cu as well studied as a skin serum?

No. Different routes of administration can produce different exposure and risks. The FDA specifically identifies limited human safety information for injectable GHK-Cu.

Does MOTS-C have proven human side effects?

Reliable human safety data are insufficient to establish a dependable list or frequency of side effects. The absence of those data is uncertainty, not reassurance.

Is sermorelin FDA-approved today?

A branded sermorelin product was historically FDA-approved for specific uses, but it is no longer marketed. Today's compounded sermorelin products are not FDA-approved, and older data should not be presented as proof of safety or efficacy for adult wellness uses.

If a peptide can be compounded under an HHS policy, does that mean the FDA has approved it?

No. The October 2026 policy concerns limited enforcement discretion under specified conditions. It is not an FDA drug approval, and it does not replace independent medical judgment.

The bottom line

It's reasonable to be interested in emerging peptide science. The right response to that interest is to ask better questions, distinguish research from proof, and be candid about what no one yet knows. For BPC-157, TB-500, injectable GHK-Cu, and MOTS-C, gaps in human safety evidence are a central part of the story. Sermorelin has more historical human-use information, but that does not establish the safety or benefits of every current compounded use.
